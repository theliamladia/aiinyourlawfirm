AI in Your Law Firm

Issue #001

Before you buy an AI tool, do this

Dorothy was our primary drafter for more than forty years, longer than any of the partners had been in the building.

One afternoon I was sitting in her office while she prepared a trust, and I watched what she did after the document was assembled. She read through some of the key provisions and meticulously made a handful of small corrections. She fixed a few spacing errors, made a couple of formatting changes, and changed every instance of “Co Trustee” by hand to “Co-Trustee.”

I asked how often she had to do that.

“Every time. It only takes a few seconds.”

I asked why she had never mentioned it.

She repeated herself. “It only takes a few seconds.”

I asked because I knew the problem could be fixed at its source. Those errors were not hers. They sat in the master forms that every one of our documents is generated from, and anything corrected there stays corrected.

So I made the change in the templates themselves. It took two minutes, in software we had already owned for about fifteen years.

From that day on, Dorothy never had to make those corrections again. She had thought of the cleanup as a few seconds, but it cost her two or three minutes on every trust. Across roughly 200 trusts a year, that is seven to ten hours of our most experienced drafter’s time, recovered every year by a two-minute change.

That fix was automation, not AI, but it turned my attention inward. For the first time, I examined, step by step, how our firm operated and how our staff spent each day. Much of that work was inefficient, a good deal of it was wasted effort outright, and nearly all of it could be done faster and more reliably.

I began systematizing everything we touch, and I divided it into two halves. The front stage is everything a client experiences: the first phone call, the design meeting, the letter that arrives afterward, the signing. The backstage is every process that must run for the front stage to work: the intake notes, the scheduling, the drafting and review, the funding follow-up, the reminder that goes out before the meeting. Clients judge us by the front stage, but everything they experience there, and all the value we provide, begins backstage. Dorothy’s hyphen was a backstage problem. No client ever saw it, but it quietly cost us hours every year.

At the time, I had no idea I was building a gold mine. When capable AI finally arrived, those documented processes were precisely the foundation it required, and they have since grown into 114 automations and AI agents that let us work faster, deliver more value to our clients, and respond to them sooner.

You will read a great deal about the front stage and the backstage in this newsletter. I first learned the terms from B. Joseph Pine II and James H. Gilmore’s book The Experience Economy, which I recommend below. Most of what we have built, and most of what we will show you, lives backstage. I want you to start thinking of your own practice the same way, as a front stage and a backstage.

Document first, then add AI

Automation performs an identical task the same way every time, like correcting that hyphen. AI handles work that requires judgment, such as reviewing a file and determining which matters need attention today. Most of what slows a law office down belongs to the first category, which means much of the improvement available to you requires no AI at all.

Most firms approach AI backwards. They purchase a tool and then attempt to reorganize the office around it, which is extraordinarily difficult to do well. Reverse the order: fit AI into the workflow you already have, altering that workflow as little as possible.

None of that is possible until the workflow is written down. Everything begins with documentation, and you can begin this week.

One thing to try

Choose one person in your firm and one task that person performs regularly, or one you perform yourself, and document it from beginning to end without omitting anything.

The goal is to capture every question a new hire would otherwise have to ask. Where does the task begin, and how do you know? What are you looking for? Which systems do you open? Which documents do you pull, and from where: the online file, the CRM, notes stored elsewhere, your email?

Record every step in sequence, including the ones that seem too small to mention, because those are usually where the time goes.

You can record the process on screen or write it out by hand. Loom captures your screen and voice and is free to start. Scribe builds a step-by-step guide as you click. A legal pad works as well.

If you record, narrate as you go, and listen carefully for Dorothy’s phrase. Wherever someone says “it only takes a few seconds,” there is almost certainly waste worth examining.

The latest in AI

A price war broke out. Claude Opus 5.5, GPT-6 Sol and GPT-6 Luna were released within days of one another, and prices fell again. Why it matters to you: the work that was once too expensive to hand to a machine is the lengthy work, such as reviewing a complete trust, working through a funding file, or reading a year of notes on a single client. That category of work just became considerably cheaper.

Suggested reading and viewing

The Experience Economy, by B. Joseph Pine II and James H. Gilmore, the book behind the front stage and backstage language. Read it if you want to think about your clients’ experience as deliberately as you think about their documents.

Matt Wolfe, a good source of AI news. He shows each tool working on screen instead of describing it in the abstract.
